Scoring Content for Review: A Refresh-Priority Model on FlyRank Search Data

Which pages should a content team look at first, when they can only review a handful each week — and can a simple learned model outrank a hand-written triage rule at picking them?

Ishi Gupta AI Fluency / ML Track — FlyRank Internship September 2026
ABSTRACT

This paper asks a narrow, practical question: given a limited weekly review budget, which content pages should a team check first for staleness and declining search performance? Using an anonymized slice of FlyRank's search-performance data, I built a hand-written triage rule as a baseline, then trained a shallow decision tree on the same signals to rank pages by likelihood of decline. Evaluated under a client-level holdout split — so no client appears in both training and test data — the model ranked true decliners into its top 50 candidates at a rate of 0.68, compared to 0.38 for the hand-written rule. The result is a ranked, human-reviewed action queue with reason codes, not an automated publishing decision. Every recommendation here is decision-support for a person, evaluated on one sample and one split, and framed with that limit in mind throughout.

01

Introduction

A content team with thousands of published pages and limited review time faces the same question every week: which pages actually need attention right now? Checking every page by hand does not scale, and simple heuristics — "anything untouched for six months" — tend to either flood the queue with pages that are fine, or miss pages that are quietly losing visibility.

This paper frames that as a Refresh / Content Opportunity Scoring problem: rank pages so a limited review budget goes to the pages most likely to need protect, improve, rewrite, or monitor action — and measure whether a learned model actually beats a transparent, hand-written rule at that ranking task, rather than assuming it does.

02

Data

Two releases of the FlyRank ML Internship dataset were used. Model training and evaluation in this paper use the anonymized starter release — roughly 30,000 content pages, 44 columns, covering a 90-day rolling performance window per page. Earlier exploratory work (data-contract validation) additionally queried the full internship warehouse release (~79M daily performance rows across clients and content items, partitioned by month) to confirm grain, availability, and feature timing before committing to the smaller sample for modeling.

What was excluded, and why

  • Rows without confirmed search-data availability were excluded rather than treated as zero-performance — missing search-console access is not the same as a page earning zero visibility.
  • No client names, raw URLs, private search queries, or credentials appear anywhere in this paper, its notebooks, or its outputs — all identifiers used are pseudonymized hashes from the released dataset.
  • The most recent month available in the full warehouse was deliberately treated as a sealed test window during exploration, not used to develop label logic, since it is the natural outcome window for any past-to-future label definition.
03

Methodology

Label definition

There is no ground-truth "this page needs review" label in the data. I used a proxy: a page is labeled declining if its recorded 30-day trend direction is downward. This is an imperfect stand-in for the real business outcome — it says a page's short-term trend moved down, not that a review would meaningfully help it — and every claim in this paper is scoped to that proxy, not to "true" content quality.

Features

Seventeen page-level signals were used as model inputs, spanning visibility (impressions, clicks, sessions), position, CTR, engagement (scroll rate, engagement rate), content attributes (word count, character count, content age), and recency (days since last update). trend_direction and trend_pct — the columns the label is directly derived from — were deliberately excluded from the feature set to avoid direct leakage.

Baseline

The baseline is a transparent hand-written rule: a page scores higher if it is stale (no update in 180+ days) and visible (500+ impressions in the last 90 days), with visibility and recency as tie-breakers. This mirrors the kind of rule a content team might write by hand before any modeling is involved.

Model and validation design

A shallow decision tree (max depth 5) was trained on the same features, using its predicted probability of decline as the ranking score. Critically, the train/test split was grouped by client, not random: roughly 20% of clients were held out entirely, so no page from a test-set client was ever seen during training. This is deliberately more conservative than a random row-level split, because a random split can let near-duplicate pages from the same client leak signal between training and test.

Leakage audit. Beyond excluding the label-derived columns, a stress test compared this client-grouped split against a naive random split on the same data and features to check whether client grouping was actually changing the result, not just adding complexity for its own sake. The full comparison, including the exact numbers from that audit, is in w06_validation_audit.ipynb in the accompanying repository — the client-grouped split was kept as the reported result throughout this paper because it is the more honest test of ranking pages for clients the model has not seen before.

Where the model leans

Feature-importance inspection shows the tree relies most on 90-day impressions, content age, and average position — a combination the hand-written rule could not weigh jointly. Error review found the model's mistakes cluster around pages with mid-range signals (moderately stale, moderately visible), where the proxy label itself is least confident — a place a human reviewer's judgment is genuinely needed, not a spot to force a cleaner threshold.

05

Limitations & Honest Framing

Proxy label, not ground truth. "Declining" here means a 30-day trend direction, not a verified business outcome. A page can be mislabeled by this proxy without the model being wrong.

Single sample, single split. Results are observed on one ~30,000-row anonymized sample and one client-grouped split. Precision@50 on a different month, client mix, or sample size is not guaranteed to match 0.68.

Correlational, not causal. Feature importance describes what the model leans on to separate this sample's labels — it is not evidence that changing any one signal (e.g. updating a page) will cause a specific outcome.

Unbalanced underlying panel. The full warehouse this sample is drawn from has uneven per-client history depth; some clients have materially shorter tracked windows than others.

Decision-support only. Every output in this paper is meant to narrow a human reviewer's list, not to trigger any automated content change. See Section 07 for explicit no-go cases.

06

Ranked Recommendations

The model's output was converted into an action queue: each page gets a score, a reason code explaining why it was flagged, and a suggested action a reviewer can start from.

STALE_HIGH_VISIBILITY
→ Rewrite

Page still earns real traffic but hasn't been touched in 180+ days — the clearest refresh candidate.

LOW_CTR_STRONG_POSITION
→ Improve

Ranking is fine, clicks aren't following — usually a snippet or title problem, not a content rewrite.

AGING_ASSET
→ Protect

Older page approaching the risk window before signals turn — a watch flag, not yet urgent.

MODEL_FLAGGED_RISK
→ Improve

Flagged by the learned model on a combination the simple rules didn't catch — needs a look to confirm why.

Priority actions

  1. Route the top of the ranked queue to a human reviewer weekly, not on a fixed content-team assumption of what's stale.
  2. Treat STALE_HIGH_VISIBILITY pages as the highest-value refresh candidates — visibility already exists, so a refresh has somewhere to land.
  3. Investigate LOW_CTR_STRONG_POSITION pages as snippet/title fixes before assuming the content itself is the problem.
  4. Track Precision@50 monthly against the 0.68 baseline established here; treat a sustained drop as a retrain trigger, not a one-off blip.
WHAT SHOULD NOT BE AUTOMATED
  • Auto-rewriting, auto-publishing, or auto-deleting any page based on this score alone
  • Treating the ranked queue as a guarantee of SEO outcome for any individual page
  • Applying this model to a client with materially less history than the training sample without re-validating first
  • Using this queue in any client-facing decision without a person checking the specific reason code first
